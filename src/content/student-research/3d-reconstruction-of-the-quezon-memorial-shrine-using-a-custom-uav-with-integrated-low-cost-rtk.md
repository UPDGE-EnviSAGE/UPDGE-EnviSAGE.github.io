abstract: |-
  3D reconstruction of buildings has been a growing trend in visualization and digital photogrammetry in the recent years (Suveg & Vosselman, 2000). Many studies are currently focusing on the importance of 3D reconstruction in its applications in mapping, surveillance, architecture, and urban planning. With the fast advancement of drone and camera technologies, image-based 3D reconstruction techniques are gaining popularity among professionals. Outputs from these methods can produce highly detailed, accurate, and photo-realistic 3D models. This method is relatively less expensive and less tedious compared to other techniques in 3D reconstruction, like laser ranging methods. In this study, a 3D model of the Quezon Memorial Shrine is reconstructed using a custom UAV with integrated low-cost RTK. The methodology was divided into three general parts, data acquisition through aerial and ground surveys, data processing by 3D modeling, and accuracy assessment. 3D modeling was done using the Agisoft PhotoScan software, which can perform automated image-based 3D reconstruction. An accuracy assessment of the model was done by comparing the absolute positional coordinates of the check points in the virtual model to their corresponding positions in the real world. An initial RMSE of 8.403 meters was obtained from the model, which indicates that this cannot be used in general surveying purposes. The individual errors in Nothings, Eastings, and elevation show that there is a systematic and translational error in positioning. This can be attributed to the accuracy and capability of the low-cost RTK receiver used. After the correction of the positional errors by shifting the model, an RMSE of 0.179 meters was obtained. The results after correction show that a high absolute positional accuracy can be obtained by adjustment if the error is translational and systematic. The lengths between check points are also checked to assess the accuracy of the model itself. Distances on the model are measured and were compared to the actual distances measured in the ground survey. With a largest absolute error of 3.7 cm and accurate measurements of up to a millimeter of error, the model produced is accurate enough for high accuracy measurements, and the methodology can be employed in as-built surveys.
